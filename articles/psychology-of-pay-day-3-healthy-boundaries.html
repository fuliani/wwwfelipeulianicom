The Boundary Review Every People-Manager Role Needs

Scope creep rarely shows up as a complaint. It shows up as an unpaid Sunday. Here’s the review that catches it before the gap becomes permanent.

Felipe Uliani · July 2026 · 3 min read
Articles › Job Architecture › The Psychology of Pay

Scope creep rarely shows up as a complaint. It shows up as an unpaid Sunday — a manager quietly absorbing more responsibility with no corresponding change to their role.

Where This Shows Up

It happens gradually, one small addition at a time: a people-manager takes on a project no one else had capacity for, then another, then starts covering a gap in a neighboring team. None of it looks like a job change in the moment. But a few quarters later, the role has expanded well past its documented scope, while the title, level, and pay band stayed exactly where they started. The job architecture never caught up to the job.

The Practice

The practice is a workload-based leveling review, triggered by one condition: whenever a manager’s actual scope has expanded beyond their documented role for more than one quarter, the review kicks in automatically. It’s deliberately not framed as a performance conversation — it’s a scope conversation:

  • Has the role’s actual responsibility grown beyond what the current level describes?
  • Is the person doing work that matches the next level up, without the level or pay to match?
  • If yes, does the fix belong in redistribution of the added work, or in a formal re-leveling?

The Proof

In my experience, the managers absorbing the most invisible scope creep are rarely the ones who raise it. They’re the ones who keep saying yes, quarter after quarter, until the gap between their actual job and their documented one becomes hard to close cleanly.

The Reframe

Healthy boundaries in leadership aren’t about saying no more often. They’re about a process that keeps scope and pay honest with each other before the gap becomes normal.
