The Calendar Review Every Newly Promoted Manager Needs

The promotion changes your level on day one. It rarely changes your week. Here’s the review that shows which job you’re actually doing.

Felipe Uliani · September 2026 · 3 min read
Articles › HR Leadership › What Nobody Teaches New Leaders

The promotion changes your level on day one. Your calendar can take a year to notice — and until it does, you’re paid for one job while doing another.

Where This Shows Up

Most job architectures draw the same line between a senior individual contributor and a manager. One level is accountable for its own output. The next is accountable for the output of others. It’s one sentence on paper, and it’s the biggest change on the whole career ladder. The new manager’s week doesn’t change with it. They still build the model, still fix the deck the night before, still take the hardest analysis because they’re the fastest at it. It doesn’t look like a problem. It looks like commitment. But the team’s work now waits behind one calendar, and the work only a manager can do gets whatever time is left.

The Practice

The practice is a calendar review, done once a month for the first year. Take the last two weeks and sort every block into three groups:

  • Work only I can do at this level: setting priorities, developing people, removing obstacles.
  • Work someone on my team could do with some support from me.
  • Work I kept because I’m good at it and it feels productive.

The third group is the one to watch. It’s usually the largest, and it’s the job you were promoted out of. Each month, hand over one item from it — with the context, not just the task.

The Proof

In my experience, when a newly promoted manager is struggling, the problem is rarely capability. Put their calendar next to the description of their level and you’ll find two different jobs — and the old one is winning.

The Reframe

A promotion isn’t a reward for the work you were doing. It’s a different job, and the calendar is where you find out whether you’ve started it.
