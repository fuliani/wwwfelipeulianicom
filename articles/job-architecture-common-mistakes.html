Job Architecture

The 5 Job Architecture Mistakes That Create Pay Equity Nightmares

Bad architecture isn’t just an HR problem. It is a legal and retention problem. These are the five structural errors I see most often — and each one is preventable.

Felipe Uliani · June 2026 · 7 min read
Articles › Job Architecture › Common Mistakes

Pay equity analyses are supposed to surface pay gaps. But in many organizations, the analysis arrives at a conclusion that is technically accurate and practically useless: “We have no statistically significant pay disparities by gender or race when we control for job level and function.”

The problem is in the phrase “when we control for job level and function.” If the job architecture is itself inequitable — if the leveling decisions, the job family definitions, or the scope descriptions embed the very disparities the analysis is trying to find — then controlling for it does not solve the problem. It hides it.

I have seen this pattern enough times to say with confidence: most pay equity problems have a job architecture root cause. Here are the five structural mistakes that generate them.

Mistake 1: Level Inflation in Specific Functions or Demographics

Level inflation happens when people get titled and leveled above what their actual scope and complexity warrant. This is almost always well-intentioned — a manager wants to pay someone more, or retain them, or signal their value to the organization, and inflating the level is the path of least resistance.

The equity problem emerges when level inflation is not evenly distributed. If it happens more frequently in majority-demographic populations, or in functions with historically stronger manager advocacy, the result is a structural pay advantage that shows up not as a direct pay disparity but as a level disparity. The pay equity analysis will show that employees at the same level are paid equitably. It will not show that some employees are at higher levels than their scope warrants while others doing comparable work are at lower levels.

The fix requires regular level auditing — comparing actual scope and responsibilities against level criteria, not just reviewing the title and pay. This is uncomfortable work. It requires managers to defend their leveling decisions against objective criteria, and some of those decisions will not hold up.

Mistake 2: Job Families That Are Defined by Incumbent Rather Than by Work

This is one of the most common architecture problems I encounter in organizations that have grown quickly. Job families get created to accommodate specific people rather than to describe categories of work. The result is a proliferation of job families with one or two incumbents, often designed to justify a specific compensation arrangement.

From a pay equity perspective, this is dangerous for two reasons. First, small job families are impossible to analyze statistically — a job family with three people cannot support a pay equity regression. Second, the decisions about who goes into which job family often have demographic patterns that are difficult to defend when scrutinized.

Job families should describe categories of work that exist independent of who is doing them. If removing the incumbents makes the job family definition meaningless, the job family is wrong.

A useful diagnostic: take your job architecture and ask whether each job family would make sense if every current incumbent left tomorrow. If the answer is no for some families, those families are defined by person, not by work.

Mistake 3: Inconsistent Level Criteria Across Functions

Most organizations develop level criteria function by function. Engineering writes its own leveling guide. Finance writes its own. Marketing writes its own. The result is a set of function-specific frameworks that are internally coherent but mutually inconsistent.

The most common inconsistency: what constitutes a “senior” individual contributor varies dramatically across functions. In some functions, “senior” means five years of experience and independent project ownership. In others, it means ten years of experience, cross-functional influence, and demonstrated thought leadership. These are not the same thing, and when they map to the same pay band, you get real equity problems.

The fix is not to force all functions into identical criteria — a senior engineer and a senior accountant genuinely have different work profiles. The fix is to establish a set of cross-functional anchors that define what “level 4” means in terms of scope, complexity, and impact, and then translate those anchors into function-specific language. The anchors provide the consistency; the translations provide the context.

Mistake 4: Manager and Individual Contributor Levels That Are Not Calibrated to Each Other

In many organizations, the manager track and the individual contributor track were designed independently. The IC track was built first, then a management track was bolted on, with level equivalencies established informally.

The equity problem this creates is particularly acute at the senior level. When a Senior Manager and a Staff Engineer (or Principal Engineer, or whatever the senior IC title is) are theoretically at the same level but one consistently earns more than the other, that disparity often has demographic patterns: management tracks have historically skewed toward one demographic, senior IC tracks toward another.

Fixing this requires explicit calibration: taking the management and IC tracks and establishing genuine equivalency criteria at each level. This is not about making the pay identical; it is about making the leveling criteria defensible and the pay differences attributable to scope and market factors rather than to which track a person happens to be on.

Mistake 5: Scope Creep Without Level Adjustment

Over time, jobs change. The scope of a role that was leveled five years ago may be dramatically larger today due to organizational growth, technology change, or shifts in business strategy. When the scope grows without a corresponding level adjustment, the employee is doing work at a higher level than they are being paid for.

This would be a compensation problem even without an equity dimension. The equity dimension emerges because scope creep without level adjustment tends to happen more to employees who are not actively advocating for themselves — and the willingness and ability to advocate is not evenly distributed across demographic groups.

The preventive measure is a regular job content review cadence, separate from the performance cycle. At least every two years, managers should be asked to review whether the actual scope of roles on their team still matches the level criteria those roles are assigned to. Many organizations only revisit job levels when there is a retention crisis, which means the adjustment comes too late and creates a reactivity pattern rather than a systematic one.

A useful test for your own architecture: Take five roles at the same level in different functions. Write down, for each one, the actual scope of work — budget managed, team size, decision authority, external impact. Now ask: are these genuinely at the same level of complexity and contribution? If the answer is no, you have a consistency problem that will show up in your pay equity analysis.

The Common Thread

All five of these mistakes share a common cause: job architecture decisions get made locally, informally, and under pressure, without reference to a consistent standard. Over years, these local decisions accumulate into a structure that looks coherent from the outside but contains systematic inconsistencies that produce inequitable outcomes.

The fix is not a one-time job architecture redesign. Those projects are necessary, but they address the symptoms rather than the cause. The cause is the absence of governance — a clear process for making leveling decisions, a designated owner for architecture consistency, and a regular audit mechanism that surfaces drift before it becomes a legal problem.

Pay equity is an outcome. Job architecture is the input. Fix the input first.
